1. Summary

The agent diagnoses which of eight documented misconceptions a student holds about the Python function-call notional machine. An LLM interviewer (a ReAct loop in LangGraph) chooses probes through constrained tools. The application keeps an exact Bayesian belief over all 256 possible profiles, and a second LLM interprets the student’s free-text explanations.

Refinement. The HW-11 baseline showed that the interpreter discarded students’ admissions of careless errors. This week’s refinement has two parts:

  • D34: the interpreter counts an admitted careless error as evidence.
  • D38: a follow-up correction found by failure analysis of D34.

On the same 100 simulated students with the live interpreter, the refined agent reached 91% exact profile match, up from 85%. Six students improved and none got worse, and certain-but-wrong diagnoses fell from 5 to 4. With the full LLM interviewer, exact match rose from 84% to 89%.

Evaluation. A pre-registered protocol defined eight matched comparisons on 12 to 500 simulated students per configuration, with seven retained cases. The refined agent meets the protocol’s provisional criteria for use within a defined scope (a research tool or a supervised pilot). No real students have been tested, and that limits every conclusion.

3. Implemented Refinement

3.1 Evidence from the HW-11 baseline and the first change (D34)

In the HW-11 baseline, the only certain-but-wrong diagnosis was sim-007. The cause was the live interpreter, which marked 10 of 11 careless-error explanations (“I put the lines in the wrong order by mistake”) as non-informative. They were discarded, so a slip that landed on a misconception’s answer was never counted against that misconception.

D34 change: the interpreter prompt now treats an admitted careless error as informative with no misconception. Prediction: fewer certain-but-wrong diagnoses for students who slip.

3.2 Failure analysis of D34 and the second change (D38)

The protocol tested D34 first in isolation: the code decider with the live interpreter, on the same 100 students. The change fixed its mechanism, with careless-error explanations judged correctly in 65 of 66 cases, up from 4 of 76. Exact match held at 85%. But certain-but-wrong diagnoses rose from 5 to 11, which failed the protocol’s acceptance rule.

Following the lecture’s failure-analysis workflow, six of the seven new cases shared one pattern:

  1. The student holds a misconception.
  2. They slip away from its predicted answer and give an unexpected answer.
  3. They admit the slip.
  4. The scoring rule (D27) counts an explanation after an unexpected answer against every misconception the probe tests, so the admission became evidence that the student holds none.

D38 change: after an unexpected answer, an explanation with no misconception labels does not update the belief. After an answer that matched a misconception’s key, it still counts against that misconception, as D34 intended. Offline on 500 students, exact match rose from 75% to 80%, and certain-but-wrong diagnoses fell from 96 to 65. D38 was decided after seeing the C3 data, and the protocol records it as a post-hoc change.

4. Evaluation Design

The protocol (docs/evaluation-protocol.md) was committed before any results. It fixes the success criteria, the comparisons, the measures, the decision rules and the rules for choosing retained cases. Every later change is dated in its change table, with its reason.

  • Task and environment. Simulated students with a planted profile; each misconception is planted independently with probability 0.2, using seed 7, so student sim-NNN is identical in every configuration.
    • Bank students answer from the answer keys with a 15% slip rate, give confidence ratings, give vague or hedged answers 15% of the time, and explain from a labeled bank of 94 explanations.
    • LLM students are Gemma playing a planted profile with a beginner persona and a style (clear, terse or hedging).
  • Success (per session): the session ends DIAGNOSED or BUDGET, and its reported profile (misconceptions with probability > 0.5) equals the planted profile exactly.
  • Mandatory requirement: no certainty on a wrong answer. Certain but wrong is a session that ends DIAGNOSED with a wrong profile. It is reported beside every success rate and is never offset by it.
  • Unsupported input succeeds when it ends with an explicit ERROR and a retained trace.
Table 2. Measures (↑ higher is better, ↓ lower is better).
MeasureDefinition
Exact match ↑Successful sessions / evaluated sessions (errors included)
Certain but wrong ↓Sessions ending DIAGNOSED with a wrong profile
Brier score ↓Mean over sessions and misconceptions of (probability − planted 0/1)²
Calibration error ↓Count-weighted mean of |mean probability − observed share planted| over six probability bins
Paired outcomePer student: both succeed / only one configuration succeeds / neither
Pass-to-fail rate ↓Students who succeed on the original input and fail on the variant, divided by the original’s successes
Cost ↓Model calls per session; model seconds per session (median, 95th percentile); model calls per success
ReliabilityERROR and rejected calls; reproducibility as agreement between reruns of the same configuration

Comparisons (same students, budget, stopping rule and scoring in each):

  • C1: adaptive vs. fixed quiz.

  • C4: the refinement in the full agent.
  • C5: interviewer model.

Protocol changes, all logged before the affected data except D38, which is marked post hoc:

  • One revision round for the LLM-student personas.
  • C7 limited to profiles the simulator can play.
  • Night-1 interviewer runs repeated after the budget cap cut them off.

5.1 Calibration of agent certainty and subgroups

For the refined agent with the Qwen interviewer, the 800 probabilities (100 students × 8 misconceptions) are well calibrated, with a calibration error of 0.007:

  • Of 154 probabilities averaging 98%, 100% were truly planted.
  • Of 611 averaging 0.9%, 1.0% were planted.
  • The middle bins are small (2–22 probabilities each) and slightly overconfident; for example, 9 at 86% were 78% planted.

Offline (4,000 probabilities), the calibration error is 0.009. Exact match by number of planted misconceptions (refined agent, Qwen, 100 students):

  • none: 15/16;
  • one: 30/30;
  • two: 22/28;
  • three or more: 22/26.

Students with several misconceptions are the harder group, as the offline 500 also show (76% and 75%).

7. Variation, Failures and Limitations

  • Simulated students only. Bank students answer from the agent’s own answer keys, so their results are an upper bound.
    • LLM students reduce this circularity but did not remove it. They were perfectly consistent (no careless errors at all) and could not play A2 or F5.
    • No real student has been tested.
  • Remaining failure modes:
    • repeated careless errors onto the same misconception’s answer (R4);
    • students with several misconceptions (75–85% exact);
    • vague answers, which change individual outcomes in both directions (pass-to-fail 16–21%).
  • Variation. Each live configuration ran once. Reruns disagree on about one student in twenty to thirty (C8), so the C4 and C5 differences of a few students are near this level. The C3 result (6 gained, 0 lost on paired students, with a code decider) is the most reliable comparison.
  • Scope. Eight catalog misconceptions, 15 probes, a 10-probe budget, two course models, one notional machine.
    • The agent cannot diagnose a misconception outside the catalog.
    • The answer keys allow one form of each wrong answer, while LLM students sometimes expressed F5 in other forms.
  • Process. D38 was decided after seeing the C3 data. It was then re-evaluated on the same students, and the protocol marks it post hoc. The $2-per-hour key cap shaped run sizes and required the paced harness.
